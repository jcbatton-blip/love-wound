Why Do I Feel Anxious When I Have More Freedom?

By Jeff Batton · November 22, 2026

They took the fence down, and the kindergartners huddled in the middle of the playground, terrified to move. I have never forgotten that little test. They put the kids out with a fence around the playground, and the kids played and had the biggest time. Then they took the kids back inside, took the fence down, and put them back outside. Same playground. No fence. And nobody ran free. Freedom did not scare them because it was dangerous. It scared them because nobody had taught them to trust themselves without a fence.

Friend, that is most of us at sixty. We pray for the fence to come down. More time, more freedom, fewer people telling us what to do. Then the fence actually comes down, the job ends, the kids are grown, the schedule opens up, and instead of running free we stand in the middle of the playground wondering why we feel anxious. We tell ourselves something must be wrong with us. Nothing is wrong with you. You just learned to feel safe inside a fence, and nobody taught you how to feel safe without one.

I know this one from the inside. Give me a full calendar and a list of people who need me and I can perform all day long. I am good at the fence. Take the fence away and give me a wide open Tuesday with nobody asking for anything, and my brain starts looking for a wall to lean on. It will invent a worry, replay an old conversation, or go find somebody to rescue, just to feel located again. I used to think that was ambition. Most days it was just a man who did not yet trust himself in open space.

Here is the plain truth under it. The fence was never what kept those kids safe. The playground did not change. What changed was their sense of where the edges were. When you cannot see an edge, your mind draws the danger bigger than it is. That is why freedom can feel like falling. Your old limits, even the ones you complained about for years, told you who you were and where you stood. Without them, you have to decide that for yourself, maybe for the first time, and deciding for yourself is the very work most of us spent a lifetime avoiding by staying busy inside the fence.

Underneath all of it is this: your brain wants certainty to stay safe. That is its job, and it does it faithfully. But life does not happen in certainty. Life happens in uncertainty, it always has. I have come to believe the quality of your life is directly proportional to the amount of uncertainty you can withstand. That is why a miserable fence can feel safer than an open field. We would rather stay in the hell we know than risk the heaven we don't.

So the answer is not to beg for the fence back. Lord knows I have tried rebuilding a few fences out of obligation, worry, and other people's expectations, and a homemade fence is just a cage with your own name on the gate. The answer is slower and kinder than that. You learn the playground. You walk a little further out each day and find out the ground holds. You let the anxiety say its piece without letting it pick your direction. You discover that the terror was never about the size of the space. It was about how little you trusted the person standing in it.

And something lovely happens on the other side of that learning. The mirror starts reflecting something different. A person who trusts himself does not need a fence to play, and he does not need a crowd to prove he is free. He can work, rest, say yes, say no, and enjoy an ordinary afternoon without huddling in the middle of his own life. That is not recklessness. That is a grown man or woman finally using the whole playground they were given.

Why Do I Keep Trying to Convince My Partner?

By Jeff Batton · November 19, 2026

You have explained it calmly. You have explained it louder. You have explained it with a list, in the car, at midnight, and once more the next morning in case the first six versions were unclear. And still he does not see it. Still she does not get it. So let me save you some breath: the problem is not your explanation. The problem is the rope in your hands.

Every argument like this is a tug of war. He pulls, you pull. You say, "Yes, but you need to do this," and that is pulling the rope. He digs in, because that is what a person does when somebody is pulling on them. Nobody in the history of rope has ever been pulled into agreeing. They just get better at resisting, and you get more tired, and the thing you actually needed never gets done.

Here is what I tell people in my sessions, most weeks, because this is the exact spot so many of them are standing in:

The best way to win a tug of war is not to pull the rope. Let go of the rope.

And before you ask, no, letting go is not giving up, and it is not losing. It is the only move that ends the game.

Now, why do we pull so hard? Because underneath the pulling there is an insecurity talking. I say it this way: the insecurity will always explain, it will always defend, and it will always look for validation. You are not really trying to win the point. You are trying to get his buy in so you can feel like you are not doing something wrong. If he would just see it, you could relax. Friend, that is a contract he never signed. I call it a silent contract: you never said it out loud, but you expect him to just get it. They rarely do. And waiting on his getting it will wear you slap out.

So what does letting go look like in a real kitchen, with a real person having a real reaction? It looks like a question instead of a case. Not pulling the rope sounds like this: "What are you feeling right now? What is happening for you right now?" Then you stop talking. He may still have his reaction. You cannot control that, and you never could. You only thought you could, if you explained it one more way, a better way.

And here is the part nobody believes until they try it. Every time you pull, he never has to see anything, because resisting you is a full time job. But if you keep letting go of the rope, eventually he runs out of battle. In my sessions I put it plainer: eventually he gets tired of hitting his butt on the floor. Words don't teach. Only experience teaches. You can say it a hundred times: don't touch the stove. But once he touches it, he's got it. That is not cruelty. That is respect. You are treating him like a grown man who can learn from his own life instead of your lectures.

Meanwhile, something quieter happens to you. The fatigue starts to lift, because the fatigue was never really about the task. It was about carrying his part too, explaining it, defending it, managing his feelings about it. Put his part down. Do what you know needs doing. And the only reason you needed his buy in to begin with was the insecurity. Because if you knew that you knew, you wouldn't need him to see it anyway. You do not need his buy in. You do not need anybody to buy in. You need your own life back, and it starts the day the rope hits the ground.

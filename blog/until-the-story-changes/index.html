Until the Story Changes

By Jeff Batton · October 19, 2026

You can say, I forgive you. You can say it out loud. You can say it in prayer. You can say it ten times before breakfast and mean every word.

And then their name comes up at lunch, and your jaw tightens. The whole old movie starts playing again, scene by scene, exactly the way it played last month and last year.

That is the tell.

I tell people this all the time: you can say, I forgive you, I forgive you, I forgive you. But until the story changes, you have not forgiven them.

Forgiveness is not a sentence you repeat until it sticks. It is the story you tell yourself about what happened, and about who you are now that it happened.

Most of us are not carrying the event itself anymore. The event ended. What we carry is the story wrapped around it. He always does this. She never cared. I was a fool to trust them. We rehearse that story so often that we stop noticing it is a story. It just feels like the truth.

And there are really two stories running at once. There is the story about what happened. And there is the story about the person who did it.

Most of us keep that second story very simple. They are selfish. They are cruel. They never cared. That may even be partly true. But a simple story about them quietly becomes a brutal story about you. You were easy to hurt. You were not worth more. You should have known better.

Then we wonder why saying the words changes nothing. The words sit on top. The story runs underneath, driving your body, your mood, and your next decision.

Please hear me on what forgiveness is not. It is not saying what happened was fine. It was not fine. It is not pretending you were not hurt. You were. It is not an invitation to hand someone the same access they had before. Forgiveness is not approval, and it is not amnesia.

Forgiveness is release. It is you deciding that this person, and this chapter, do not get to narrate the rest of your life.

So how does the story actually change?

First, you tell it honestly. Not the cleaned up version you perform for other people, and not the harshest version you perform at two in the morning. Just what happened, what it cost you, and what you made it mean about you. That last part is where the lock is. Much of the pain is not only in what they did. It is in what you decided it proved about your worth.

Second, you change the story you are telling about the other person.

You start by getting curious. Not generous. Not soft. Curious.

What got them here? What had life already taught them about love, trust, power, and pain before they ever got to you? What were they afraid of? What were they protecting? What did they simply not know how to do?

You may never know the answers. You are not trying to read their mind, and you are not building them a defense. You are practicing a wider lens. They were not only a character in your story. They were living inside one of their own. People make choices from the map they have. Sometimes that map is wounded. Sometimes it is distorted. Sometimes it is cruel. Seeing the map does not make the road right. It explains how they kept driving it.

That is where the thing starts to depersonalize. Their action stops being evidence about your value. It becomes information about their capacity, their history, and their limits. It may still be unacceptable. It may still have cost you dearly. But it no longer gets to name you.

Depersonalizing is not excusing. It is refusing to keep translating someone else’s behavior into a verdict on your worth.

And here is the part nobody likes. As long as the story keeps you only in the victim’s chair, the ego stays in charge. The ego would rather be right about the injury than free from it. It gets to be innocent. It gets to be righteous. It gets to keep the case open. That is not a condemnation of anyone who has been hurt. Being hurt is real. But there is a difference between honoring the wound and building your home inside it.

Third, you let understanding and boundaries live in the same house. Their choices belong to them. Their history may help explain them, but it does not transfer the responsibility. Your work is still your story, your boundaries, and your next step. You can see a person more clearly and still say, You do not get this kind of access to me again. In fact, when the story is less personal, the boundary gets cleaner. It is not revenge. It is not a performance. It is just true.

Fourth, you practice a truer story until it feels like yours. Something plain. This happened. It hurt. They acted from the story they were carrying. It was never the measure of my worth. I can release them without reopening the door.

You will not believe it the first ten times you say it. Say it anyway. A new story gets stronger the same way the old one did, by repetition.

One day their name will come up and you will notice something quiet. No surge. No movie. Just a fact from a chapter that is closed. That is not you going numb. That is the story having changed.

You do not have to force that day today. You only have to stop expecting the words alone to do work that only a new story can do.
